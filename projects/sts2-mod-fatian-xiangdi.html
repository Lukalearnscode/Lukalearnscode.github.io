A Card for the Ironclad

October 2026 · Slay the Spire 2 v0.111.0 · macOS

The card is called Fa Tian Xiang Di, after a move in Chinese myth where a fighter calls up a giant form of himself. The Ironclad plays it and a red war god rises behind him. At the end of every turn after that, the war god throws one punch and every enemy loses 100 HP. It costs 1 energy.

I don't write code. Claude wrote the code, and GPT painted the art that ended up in the game. My part came down to three things: saying what I wanted, playing it, and saying what looked wrong.

I first gave the animation to Codex. My verdict on what came back was that it "only just met my expectations", some way short of the impact I was after. Late on October 6 I handed everything to Claude. Once a first version was playable we went through nine more rounds of changes, and it was the morning of the 7th before we were done.

This page is about those nine rounds. There are already write-ups of the modding interface, so I won't repeat them. What I wanted to keep is something else: where a person who doesn't code actually gets stuck.

What to do first

Claude set the order. I'm copying it here as it was:

StepDone when
1You know how the game finds a mod. In this version, it's three files in the game's mods folder
2An empty mod that does nothing shows up as loaded in the game log, with no errors
3The card and its damage work, with placeholder art
4The animation art exists
5Animation, position and sound are wired into the game
6The game can test itself (more on this below)
7I play it, complain, and it gets changed. Step 6 runs again after every round

Step 2 looks like the dull one, but until it works nothing after it can be checked. Research and project setup took about 15% of the effort this time, including a very slow download of an older Godot. The game only accepts resource packs built with Godot 4.5.1 and rejects packs from newer versions.

The numbers, on the other hand, needed no planning. I changed the damage three times, from 8 to 30 to 80 to 100, and each change took a few minutes.

What went wrong

Ordered by how much time each one cost.

Trap 01 · five rounds

The war god came out dark and oversaturated

In the game he was much darker than the source art, a red that was nearly black. Nothing reported an error and everything ran.

I said the colour was wrong. Claude ruled out texture compression, ruled out a few other things, and landed on a conclusion: the game was probably grading the whole screen. I asked twice more. On the third ask it finally tried something different. It drew a few swatches of known colour next to the war god, took a screenshot, and read the colours back off the screen.

The swatches hadn't changed at all, so the game wasn't doing it. The war god's colours were exactly the source colours multiplied by themselves. A few lines of tinting code Claude had written in round two were applying the same image's colour twice, so a red of 0.38 came out as 0.15.

What changed: Claude wrote a line to itself in its notes afterwards: "When the user says the colour is wrong, measure the pixels on screen first. Don't guess first."

Trap 02

It said it was changed, and it wasn't

In round two Claude reported that it had "removed the brightening at the one-second mark". In round four we found that the change had never taken effect.

The same animation code lived in two places: a standalone file, and a copy embedded in the web page used for exporting frames. It had edited the first. The export read the second.

What changed: before reporting "changed", confirm it in the file that actually goes into the game, not only in the source file that was edited.

Trap 03

The art style didn't match the game

The first war god was painted in a realistic, heavily rendered style. Slay the Spire 2 is flat colour with thick outlines. With both on one screen, anyone could see the war god didn't belong to this game.

I sent GPT a screenshot of the game and had it repaint the war god one part at a time: eight body parts and a tail of flame. The line in the prompt that mattered most was to change only how it was painted, and leave the position, size and outline of every part alone. That way the skeleton and the motion didn't need redoing. GPT still nudged parts around on the quiet, so Claude wrote a script to fit the new art back onto the old positions.

What changed: take a screenshot of a real fight before looking for any art, and set the style from that.

Trap 04

The image was blurry

Two causes. The animation frames were exported at 0.6 scale, while the game runs 2560 pixels wide on my screen, so each image pixel was stretched over more than two screen pixels. And the source art had nine parts packed into one sheet, about 400 pixels each.

Setting the scale back to 1.0 made it sharp. The cost was that the mod's resource pack grew from 136MB to 377MB.

What changed: check the game's real resolution on your own screen at the start, and size the art to that.

Trap 05

Dealing cards didn't feel smooth

The next day I noticed that with the mod on, the cards came out a little slowly each turn. The unmodded game was smooth. I asked Claude whether the mod was slowing the game down.

Claude measured frame rate over 24 turns first. With or without the war god it held a steady 60 frames a second, and nothing looked wrong. The real cause turned up later. When the game runs with mods it switches to a separate set of save files, the settings in that set start from defaults, and Fast Mode was off. In my normal game each card takes 0.1 seconds to fly out. Here it took 0.25.

What changed: the launch script now copies my normal settings across when the modded save set has none. This had nothing to do with performance, but I'd described it as "stutter", so frame rate is where it looked first.

Trap 06

30 damage, and the enemy lost 9

The test enemy was a beetle. The beetle has an ability that caps each hit at 9 HP.

What changed: test numbers on an enemy with no special abilities.

Letting the game test itself

Claude can't see my screen or hear my speakers. So it added a piece of test code to the mod that stays off in normal play. Drop a marker file in place and the game starts a run on its own, enters a fight, plays the card from the hand, ends the turn, takes screenshots and records audio from inside the game, writes how much HP each enemy lost to the log, and quits.

The colour bug, a flicker when the war god first appeared, and the damage numbers were all caught this way. By Claude's own estimate this part was about a tenth of the effort, and every round afterwards relied on it.

One detail worth keeping: the test has to play the real card from the hand. In the first round it used the debug console to attach the effect straight to the character, and that missed the flicker, because the two routes run through different code.

If I did it again

The nine rounds of changes were roughly 35% of all the effort. Looking back, Claude said that if the art style, the sharpness and where the war god stands had been settled against a game screenshot before starting, at least four of those rounds wouldn't have happened.

So next time I'll do one thing first: take a screenshot of the character in a fight, mark on it how big the new thing is, where it stands and what it looks like, and only then ask anyone to paint.

The other one is mine. With the colour, the first time I said "that's wrong" I didn't ask how it knew its answer. Next time it says "probably", I'll ask whether it measured.
